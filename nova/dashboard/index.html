Inside the ADHD brain, and how training reaches it

ADHD isn't a lack of effort. It's a difference in how the brain's control networks develop and signal. Neurofeedback works on the electrical side of that picture: it rewards the brain, moment by moment, for producing the rhythms of steady attention and calm control, until it finds them more easily on its own.

What's different in the ADHD brain

Control networksWeaker signalling in the brain's "manager" circuitsLoops between the prefrontal cortex and the basal ganglia plan, start, stop and sustain behaviour. In ADHD, dopamine and noradrenaline signalling in these loops is less efficient. Stimulant medicines work by boosting exactly these signals.
DevelopmentA brain that matures on a delayBrain-imaging studies found the cortex reaches peak thickness about three years later in children with ADHD, most of all in prefrontal regions that control attention and planning (Shaw et al., 2007).
Mind-wanderingThe "idle" network doesn't switch offThe default mode network runs daydreams and self-talk. Normally it quietens when you focus. In ADHD it keeps intruding during tasks, which shows up as lapses and drifting attention.
ImpulsesSlower, less reliable brakesStopping an action depends on the right inferior frontal cortex and its links to the basal ganglia. In ADHD, stopping is slower and less consistent. Immediate rewards also weigh more heavily than later ones, so the quick option tends to win.

What EEG shows, and what it doesn't

Many people with ADHD show more slow theta activity (4-8 Hz) and less fast beta (13-30 Hz) over the front and top of the head, a raised theta/beta ratio. This looks like under-arousal: a brain idling when it should be engaged. Many also show weaker SMR (12-15 Hz) over the motor strip, the rhythm of a still, quiet body.

This pattern holds for a subgroup, not everyone. Later studies found the theta/beta difference smaller than early ones (Arns et al., 2013). In 2013 the FDA cleared a theta/beta test only as an aid to a clinician's assessment, not as a diagnosis. Some people with ADHD show the opposite, excess beta (often with anxiety), or a slow alpha peak.

That's why NOVA never uses fixed "normal" values. Each session starts by measuring your resting levels, and thresholds adapt to you.

Across meta-analyses, standard protocols improve ADHD symptoms. Effects are clearly smaller when the raters don't know who trained (Cortese et al., 2016). The gains that do appear tend to hold at 6-12 month follow-up (Van Doren et al., 2019). Clinical courses usually run 30-40 sessions, 2-3 a week. Results take weeks, not days.

Training the brain to let impulses pass

Neurofeedback is operant conditioning. When the target state appears, the sound rewards it within half a second, and brains repeat states that earn rewards. You don't have to force it. Effortful straining usually raises muscle tension and high beta, which withholds the reward.

The Impulse Control program adds a simple practice on top of the feedback:

  1. Notice the urge: to move, check your phone, switch tasks.
  2. Name it silently: "urge".
  3. Breathe out slowly and stay still.
  4. Let it pass. The sound stays open while you hold steady; when you act on the urge, it dims.

Transfer is the point. Before homework, work or a conversation, take three breaths and recall the feel of the sound blooming. Clinics use this kind of transfer practice to carry the trained state into daily life.

Willpower, discipline and positive action

There is no single "willpower wave". Self-control comes from several systems working together, and some of them leave clear EEG signatures that can be trained:

Effort and self-controlFrontal-midline theta (Fz)The anterior cingulate monitors conflict and drives effort. When it works, a focal 4-7 Hz theta rhythm rises at the top front of the head. Training it upward improved cognitive control and working memory in healthy adults (Enriquez-Geppert et al., 2014). Willpower program.
MotivationLeft-frontal approachRelatively more activity in the left prefrontal cortex goes with approach motivation, the pull toward goals (Harmon-Jones and colleagues). Positive Action trains this balance, then left-frontal beta while you rehearse acting.
Calm readinessSMRActing from stillness rather than restlessness. The same quiet-body rhythm as Track 1 consolidates the gains.

Isn't this the opposite of lowering theta? No. The theta lowered in ADHD training is diffuse, resting slow-wave drift. Frontal-midline theta is a focal rhythm that appears during effortful control. They come from different processes.

Discipline also lives in behaviour, not just brain state. Pair each session with one concrete action you'll take afterwards, and do it within the hour.

Training on iPad with AirPods

  1. Connect your AirPods to the iPad. In Control Centre, press and hold the volume slider and turn Spatial Audio off; it remixes the stereo field.
  2. Open NOVA, choose a program and a length, and tap Listen to set a comfortable level. Keep it low; the sound is meant to sit in the background.
  3. Tap Begin session. NOVA keeps the screen awake, and the sound plays even if the silent switch is on.
  4. With a headset: the iPad can't connect to EEG headsets from the browser. Run nova app --host 0.0.0.0 on a computer the headset connects to, then open the address it prints on the iPad (same Wi-Fi). Without one, the Demo signal plays the full session with real audio.
  5. Entrainment: during training the music pulses gently at a rhythm that starts at your brain's own frequency and leads it, step by step, to the one you're training (10 Hz for alpha, 13.5 Hz for SMR, 6 Hz for frontal theta). It only moves on as fast as your brain follows. In AirPods each ear also gets a slightly different tone, so the beat lands at precisely that rate. It nudges rather than forces: the evidence for sound rhythms shifting brainwaves is mixed, and the feedback itself is the training. Turn it off under Details.

Safety and honest limits

  • NOVA is a training tool, not a medical device. It doesn't diagnose ADHD or replace treatment. Don't change medication without your prescriber.
  • Common, usually short-lived effects: tiredness, headache, irritability or trouble sleeping. Beta and willpower training late in the evening can make sleep harder. If an effect persists, shorten sessions or switch to calming programs.
  • If you have epilepsy or a history of seizures, or for training children, work with a qualified clinician.
  • Consumer headsets have few sensors, often not at the classic sites. NOVA uses the closest available ones and tells you which.
